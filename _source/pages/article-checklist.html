Moving Back to Africa: The 2026 Checklist

By Ada Eguzouwa. Updated November 2026.

Moving back home works when you treat it like a business project: plan it, budget it, test it, then do it. This checklist covers the 10 areas that decide whether your move succeeds.

1. Your mindset and your why

A plan on paper won't carry you if your mind is still full of fear, guilt or other people's opinions. Moving back is not going backwards. Social media doesn't help: it makes moving back look easier and more glamorous than it really is.

  • Can you say in one sentence why you're moving, beyond "I'm tired of it here"?
  • Are you moving toward something at home, not only running from something abroad?
  • Does your spouse or partner truly want this too?
  • Is this permanent, for a few years, or a two-home life?

2. Income

No income plan is one of the most common reasons returnees end up moving abroad again. Start here.

  • Do you have income that keeps coming after you move: a remote job, a running business, rent, a pension or contracts?
  • Is it in a strong currency like USD, CAD, GBP or EUR? If not, do you understand your exchange-rate risk?
  • If you're starting a business, is it already running, or have you tested it before moving?
  • Does your income cover your monthly costs at home without touching your savings?

3. Money and safety net

  • Save at least 12 months of living costs, separate from any business or building money.
  • Keep a "ticket back" fund, enough to return abroad if you need to.
  • Budget at today's prices, not the prices from your last visit. Rents can rise fast in areas popular with returnees, so ask what the same place cost a year ago.
  • Budget for family and community requests. They will come.
  • Talk to a tax adviser about your pension and your tax status in both countries.

4. Land, property and the people you trust

This is where a lot of diaspora money disappears. And the biggest losses rarely come from strangers. They come from people you trust: relatives, caretakers, hired professionals and even other returnees.

  • Is every property you own in your name, with documents you've checked yourself?
  • Have you confirmed the title at the land registry, not only through the seller or a relative?
  • Never send the next building payment until you've verified the last stage: receipts plus a live video call you control, or a visit from someone independent. Photos can be of someone else's building.
  • Do you have at least one person on the ground who isn't family and reports to you independently? A professional title isn't proof, so check references and registration.

Red flags

  • "Just send the money first. I'll send the documents later."
  • A relative or caretaker who discourages you from visiting or from checking the land registry yourself.
  • A developer who asks for more money before delivering what you already paid for.
  • A house sold as "close to the airport" when you haven't timed the drive yourself.

5. Family and expectations

Don't plan from afar. Go home regularly to check on your project and meet the professionals you work with. Some people say the money for a trip home is better spent on the project. It isn't: you can lose a whole investment because you wouldn't pay for a flight to check on it.

  • Have you and your family discussed your plan, your budget, and what you can and can't support?
  • Have you and your partner agreed who handles family requests, and how much you give?
  • Do you know where you'll live, beyond "with family until I sort myself out"?
  • Do you have friends or a community at home beyond your relatives?

6. Health

As a nurse, this is the section I'd never let anyone skip.

  • Find a hospital and a doctor near where you'll live, and visit them if you can.
  • Get health insurance that works there, with emergency evacuation if you can afford it.
  • Plan where to buy a genuine supply of any ongoing medications, and what it costs.
  • Know the nearest place that can handle an emergency, and how long it takes to get there.

7. Children and schooling

  • Shortlist schools and check fees, curriculum and accreditation.
  • Think about how the move affects your children's university options abroad later.
  • Visit while school is in session. If you can, let your children attend for a week or two during a holiday.
  • Prepare them for culture shock: language, discipline, and how they'll be treated as returnees.

8. Housing and daily life

You don't have to spend all your money building a house before you move. It's OK to rent. Set the rental up to your standard and see how it feels to live in that city.

  • Budget for backup power (solar, inverter or generator) and a reliable water supply, and know who to call when the gas, water or power fails.
  • Check your internet options, especially if your income depends on being online.
  • Choose an area for its security, commute, and access to a hospital and school.
  • Know how you'll get around and what it costs each month.

9. Papers, banking and ID

  • Check that your home-country passport is valid, and get the national ID you'll need. In Nigeria that's your NIN, plus a BVN for banking. In Ghana it's the Ghana Card. In Kenya it's your national ID, now being replaced by the Maisha Card. In Ethiopia it's the Fayda ID.
  • Understand what happens to your residency or citizenship abroad if you're away a long time. Keeping your status abroad keeps your freedom to travel.
  • Keep a bank account, phone number and address abroad so you can still reach your accounts and credit.
  • Scan your key documents, store them safely online, and put every expiry date in your calendar.

Plan B for paperwork. Moving to a country you weren't born in? Residency papers can take far longer than the official timeline. Decide which visa you'll live on while you wait.

10. The trial run and your first 30 days

  • Spend at least 3 months at home in the 2 years before your move, living normally, not on holiday.
  • Live on a local budget and solve everyday problems yourself.
  • Test your work setup: power, internet and time zones.
  • Write a 90-day landing plan: where you'll stay, what you'll do first, and who you'll see.
  • Set a review date 6 or 12 months in to decide: stay, adjust, or go back without shame.

What returnees say about the first month: power cuts and water problems before you know who to call, prices higher than you budgeted, paperwork slower than promised, traffic, and feeling like an outsider. Expect them, and plan for them.
